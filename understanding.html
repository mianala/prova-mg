PROVA‑MG  /  A guided climb from basic algebra

Understanding PROVA‑MG, one symbol at a time

If you can read “x + 2 = 5, so x = 3”, you already know enough to start. This page adds exactly one new idea at a time until the final, novel formula at the bottom is something you can read out loud and actually mean.

How to read this page

Every formula in the thesis is built from a handful of small ideas stacked on top of each other. We will introduce those ideas in order. Nothing below assumes more than school algebra.

Each step has three parts: the new idea in words, the symbol that stands for it, and a plain-English reading of the formula so you never have to decode it alone. A running “what we can read so far” bar tracks your progress.

The destination The goal of the whole project: teach a computer to turn Malagasy speech into written text, even though almost no labelled Malagasy audio exists. The final formula is the rule the system follows so it can teach itself from unlabelled audio without fooling itself.
Step 0

A letter is just a box that holds a value

In algebra, x is a placeholder for a number we don’t know yet. That is the only trick, and it never goes away — it only gets dressed up. In this project the placeholders hold richer things than numbers:

SymbolWhat is in the box
xthe audio — the sound recording (a chunk of speech)
ythe text — the transcription of what was said
dthe dialect (Merina, Betsimisaraka, Sakalava, …)
rthe register (read aloud, broadcast, conversation, ceremony)
cthe code‑switching tag (Malagasy, French, or mixed)

Whenever you see one of these letters, just say its plain name in your head: “x” → “the audio”, “y” → “the text”. The maths stays the same; only the contents of the boxes are bigger than a single number.

So far we can read: x, y, d, r, c — five labelled boxes.
Step 1

A function is a machine: put something in, get something out

You already met functions as f(x) — “f of x” — a machine that takes x and returns a value. Read the parentheses as “applied to”. That is all g(features) later will mean: a machine applied to some inputs.

$$f(x) = 2x + 1$$
In words “Take the box x, double it, add one.” Put in 3, get out 7. A function is a promise: same input, same output.
So far we can read: boxes, and f( · ) = a machine applied to whatever sits in the parentheses.
Step 2

Probability: a number between 0 and 1 for “how likely”

We write p(·) for “the probability of”. It is just a function whose output is always between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). 0.5 means “coin‑flip”.

$$p(y) \quad\text{means}\quad \text{“how likely is the text } y \text{?”}$$

Two short extensions you must recognise, both ordinary algebra:

  • Joint — p(x, y): “how likely is it that this audio and this text happen together?” The comma means “and”.
  • Conditional — p(y | x): “given that we already have the audio x, how likely is the text y?” The bar “|” means “given” or “assuming we already know”.
Read it out loud p(y | x) = “the chance the text is y, given the recording x.” This single object — chance of the text given the sound — is what an ASR system is ultimately trying to compute.
So far we can read: p(y) a chance, p(x,y) chance of both, p(y|x) chance of y given x.
Step 3

Multiplying chances = a chain of “and”s — the generative model

When several independent-ish things all have to happen, you multiply their chances (two coins both landing heads: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25). The thesis imagines how a recording is “born”, one factor at a time, and multiplies them. This is the first real formula from the paper:

$$p(x, y, d, r, c) = p(d)\,p(r)\,p(c \mid r, d)\,p(y \mid d, r, c)\,p(x \mid y, d, r)$$
Reading it left to right (the comma is “and”, the bar is “given”) “The chance of this whole situation = pick a dialect × pick a register × decide the language mix given dialect&register × produce the text given all that × finally produce the sound given the text, dialect and register.”

Plain comparison: it is like subtitling that simultaneously guesses what was said, in which accent, in which setting (radio? chat?), and whether the sentence mixes French and Malagasy. Each guess feeds the next, and we multiply how likely each guess is.

So far we can read: a product of chances describing how audio+text is generated. One full paper formula down.
Step 4

Logarithms: turn hard multiplication into easy addition

Multiplying many tiny probabilities gives absurdly small numbers that computers handle badly. The fix is the logarithm, written log. Its one magic property is all you need:

$$\log(A \times B) = \log A + \log B$$
Why we bother “log of a product = sum of the logs.” So every messy multiplication from Step 3 becomes a tidy addition. That is the only reason “log” appears everywhere below — it is a convenience, not a new concept.

One side note: probabilities are below 1, so their logs are negative. “Bigger” (closer to 0) still means “more likely”. Don’t let the minus signs scare you.

So far we can read: log turns × into +, so “most likely” becomes “biggest sum”.
Step 5

Decoding: add up the evidence and keep the best text

Now combine Steps 2–4. To transcribe, the system scores each candidate text by adding three kinds of evidence (thanks to log, these are sums). This is the project’s decoding rule:

$$\log P(y \mid x) \;\propto\; \log P_{\mathrm{acoustic}}(x \mid y) \;+\; \alpha \log P_{\mathrm{LM\text{-}MG}}(y) \;+\; \beta \log P_{\mathrm{typ}}(y)$$
PiecePlain meaning
Pacoustic(x|y)“does the sound match this text?”
PLM-MG(y)“is this good Malagasy on its own?”
Ptyp(y)“does it fit the cousin languages (Indonesian, Malay, Tagalog…)?”
α, βplain dials (just numbers) saying how much to trust the last two clues
∝“proportional to” — equal up to a constant we don’t care about
In words “A text’s score = how well it matches the sound + (a little) how Malagasy it sounds + (a little) how Austronesian its shape is.” The α and β dials just balance the three voices. Comparison: learning Italian faster by leaning on Spanish and Portuguese.
So far we can read: a weighted sum of log-evidence that ranks candidate transcriptions.
Step 6

argmax and argmin: “which choice wins?”

Two pieces of notation that look intimidating but mean something everyday.

  • argmax — “the input that makes this biggest.” Not the biggest value, but which choice produces it. (“Who scored the most points?” → the person, not the score.)
  • argmin — the same, but the input that makes it smallest.
$$\hat{y} = \operatorname*{argmax}_{y}\ \big[\text{score of } y\big]$$
In words “Out of all possible texts y, hand me the one with the highest score.” The little y under the word means “we are choosing among the y’s.” That is decoding: argmax over the rule from Step 5.
So far we can read: argmax = the choice that wins; argmin = the choice that loses-least.
Step 7

The average machine: “E” (expected value)

The symbol 𝔼 (a fancy E) means average — the typical outcome if you weight every possibility by how likely it is. Plain algebra: it is a weighted sum, value × chance, added up.

$$\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[ \text{something} \big] \quad=\quad \text{the average of “something”, weighted by the chances } q$$
In words “If you tried this many times following the odds q, what would you get on average?” The little q under 𝔼 just says which set of odds to average with.
So far we can read: 𝔼q[ · ] = the average of something under the odds q.
Step 8

KL: a number for “how different are two opinions?”

Often we have two sets of odds — the model’s guess q and the “true” odds p — and want to know how far apart they are. The KL divergence, written KL(q ‖ p), is that gap as a single number: 0 means identical, bigger means more disagreement.

$$\mathrm{KL}\big( q \,\|\, p \big) \;\ge\; 0$$
In words “How surprised would you be if you trusted q but reality followed p?” You don’t need its formula — only that it is a distance between two probability opinions, never negative.
So far we can read: KL(q‖p) = how far guess q is from target p (0 = perfect match).
Step 9

EM: guess the hidden parts, then improve — repeat

Here is the engine. We have audio but, for most of it, no human transcription — and we never directly see the dialect, register or language-mix. Those are hidden. The EM algorithm handles “learn from data with missing pieces” by alternating two moves until things settle:

  • E‑step (Estimate): with the current model, guess the hidden pieces — the best transcript, dialect, register, mix. Using Step 8’s tool: pick the guess‑odds q that are closest (smallest KL) to what the model believes.
  • M‑step (Maximize): now treat those guesses as if they were true labels, and adjust the model to fit them as well as possible (argmax of the average log‑likelihood from Step 7).
$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{E-step:}\quad & q^* = \operatorname*{argmin}_{q}\ \mathrm{KL}\big( q \,\|\, p_\theta(\,\cdot \mid x) \big) \\ \textbf{M-step:}\quad & \theta \leftarrow \operatorname*{argmax}_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{q^*}\big[ \log p_\theta(x, y, d, r, c) \big] \end{aligned}$$
θ(“theta”) all the knobs of the model — the things learning adjusts
q*the best current guess of the hidden pieces (the star = “the winning one”)
←“becomes” — update the knobs to this new value
In words E‑step: “with today’s knobs, make the best guess of the missing answers.” M‑step: “turn the knobs so those guesses become as likely as possible.” Loop. Comparison: a student grading their own homework to improve — each round a little better.
So far we can read the whole EM loop: guess hidden answers (E), then retune knobs θ to fit them (M), repeat.
Step 10

The danger that makes this hard: self‑delusion

A student who grades their own homework can drift into confidently marking wrong answers right, then studying from them — getting surer and worse at the same time. In machine learning this is called collapse, and it is exactly what kills naive self‑training. The novel contribution of PROVA‑MG is to bolt four guard‑rails onto the E‑step so the loop cannot fool itself.

The whole idea in one line Keep EM’s self‑teaching power, but only let it learn from guesses that are (C1) honestly confident, (C2) not a wild swing from last round, (C3) backed by other models, and (C4) shaped like a real Austronesian word.
Step 11

The four guard‑rails, each a small inequality

Each guard‑rail is just an algebra inequality (“must be at least” / “must be no more than”). They define a set of allowed guesses, written C. Read each one slowly — you already know every symbol.

  • C1 — honest confidence. Only keep a transcript if its calibrated confidence clears a bar τ (“tau”, just a threshold number).
    $$\operatorname{conf}_{\mathrm{cal}}(y, x) \;\ge\; \tau$$
    In words “Confidence in y must be at least τ — and confidence that has been reality‑checked, not just bravado.” We ignore the student who says “99% sure” but is wrong half the time.
  • C2 — no whiplash. From one round n to the next n+1, the guesses may only drift a little — the average KL gap (Step 8) stays under a small ε (“epsilon” = a tiny number).
    $$\mathbb{E}_x\big[\, \mathrm{KL}\big( q^{(n+1)} \,\|\, q^{(n)} \big) \,\big] \;\le\; \epsilon$$
    In words “On average, this round’s opinion must stay close to last round’s.” A doctor revises a diagnosis gradually, not overnight.
  • C3 — second opinions. The kept answer must also appear in the short‑lists (“n‑best”) of the independent teacher models — Whisper, MMS, Wav2Vec2‑BERT.
    $$\operatorname{supp}(q^*) \,\cap\, \big( \text{n-best}_W \cup \text{n-best}_M \cup \text{n-best}_B \big) \;\ne\; \emptyset$$
    supp(q*)the answers our guess actually considers (“support”)
    ∩“overlap with” (intersection)
    ∪“pooled together” (union)
    ≠ ∅“is not empty” — i.e. there is overlap
    In words “Our chosen text must show up on at least one other expert’s short‑list.” Keep only what independent experts also propose.
  • C4 — it must look like a real word. On average, the typological score (“does this fit Austronesian sound‑patterns?”) must stay above a floor −η (“eta”).
    $$\mathbb{E}_{q^*}\big[ \log P_{\mathrm{typ}}(y) \big] \;\ge\; -\eta$$
    In words “The kept transcripts must, on average, be phonetically plausible for the language family.” A French speaker feels “travu” could be French while “tlbvk” could not — even never having seen either. We throw out the “tlbvk” cases. (Not about whether a word exists elsewhere — Malagasy has plenty of its own words — but whether its shape is possible.)

Bundle all four into one name: C = “the set of guesses that pass C1 and C2 and C3 and C4.”

So far we can read: four plain inequalities C1–C4, and the allowed‑guess set C = all four at once.
Step 12 — The summit

The final, novel formula — read it like a sentence

Everything you have learned now snaps together. Take the EM loop from Step 9, and in the E‑step add three little words under the argmin: “q ∈ C” — “choose only among guesses that obey the four guard‑rails.” That single restriction is the contribution.

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{E-step:}\quad & q^* = \operatorname*{argmin}_{q \,\in\, C}\ \mathrm{KL}\big( q \,\|\, p_\theta(\,\cdot \mid x) \big) \\ \textbf{M-step:}\quad & \theta \leftarrow \operatorname*{argmax}_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{q^*}\big[ \log p_\theta(x, y, d, r, c) \big] + \lambda\, R(\theta) \end{aligned}$$
Now read the whole thing in plain English E‑step: “Among only the guesses that are honestly confident, not a wild swing, backed by other models, and shaped like a real word (q ∈ C), pick the one (argmin) closest (smallest KL) to what the model currently believes about the audio.”

M‑step: “Then turn the model’s knobs θ (argmax) so those trusted guesses become as likely (highest average log‑chance) as possible — plus a tidiness term λR(θ) that stops the knobs from going to extremes.”

Then loop. Because of q ∈ C, every round can only learn from trustworthy guesses, so it gets better without the self‑delusion of Step 10.
Last two new symbolsPlain meaning
q ∈ Cthe novel part: “q must be inside the allowed‑guess set C” — obey all four guard‑rails
λ R(θ)a gentle “don’t overdo it” penalty (λ = how strict, R = a tidiness score)
What makes it novel Plain EM has been known for decades. The contribution is the two characters “∈ C”: forcing the self‑teaching loop to draw only from guesses that pass four independent sanity checks — calibrated confidence, bounded drift, cross‑model agreement, and typological plausibility. That turns a loop that usually collapses into one that is provably stable for low‑resource Malagasy.
You can now read the final formula end to end:
boxes (x,y,d,r,c) → functions f(·) → chances p(·) → products of chances → logs turn × into + → weighted‑sum decoding → argmax/argmin pick the winner → 𝔼 averages → KL measures disagreement → EM guesses-then-improves → four guard‑rails C → constrained variational EM.
One‑line summary

Three teacher models + cousin‑language hints + honest confidence, combined by an EM loop that may only learn from guesses passing four guard‑rails — which is what makes its self‑training provably non‑collapsing.
