Guide to Writing a Doctoral Thesis

Guidelines and recommendations for structuring and presenting doctoral theses at the Thematic Doctoral School of Madagascar "Diversity and Sustainable Development" (EDTM 3D), University of Toamasina.

Year: 2026

This guide was designed to support doctoral candidates enrolled at the Thematic Doctoral School of Madagascar "Diversity and Sustainable Development" (EDTM 3D) at the University of Toamasina during the writing phase of their thesis. It provides guidelines and recommendations for structuring and presenting their work, and contributes to a more uniform presentation of doctoral theses at EDTM 3D.

In the same spirit, complementary documents have been produced: a Doctoral Research Guide, intended to support candidates throughout their scientific process, and a Scientific Article Writing Guide, which aims to promote the dissemination and valorization of thesis results. For any further information, candidates are invited to contact the Doctoral School or their host team.

Section 1

What is a doctoral thesis?

A doctoral thesis is a set of novel, reflective ideas resulting from theoretical, empirical, or experimental research, presented as a work of academic value (respecting academic standards) and scientific value (based on verifiable sources, referring to ideas widely recognized and tested, put forward by scientists). It is written under the supervision of a thesis director (and, where applicable, a co‑director), in a specific academic field and on a given topic.

Section 2

What is not a doctoral thesis?

A doctoral thesis is NOT:

  • A documentary collection, a literature review, or a compilation of data on a topic
  • A personal essay or dissertation presenting an individual point of view
  • A descriptive text about a phenomenon, with no research problem or research question
  • Research without an explicit methodological approach or a clear protocol
  • An internship report or a record of professional experience
  • An extended master's thesis without genuine innovation
  • A fixed or dogmatic document that leaves no room for evolving scientific debate
  • Work that lacks ethics or intellectual honesty — showing plagiarism or falsified data
Section 3

Objectives of a doctoral thesis

A thesis is a training in intellectual rigor, autonomy, and critical thinking. Writing a doctoral thesis is not merely an academic exercise but an intellectual, scientific, and personal journey.

The defense of the doctoral thesis is the decisive test for entering a scientific and academic career, as a researcher or teacher‑researcher. Its validation marks the candidate's integration into the scientific community, both nationally and internationally.

The doctoral journey addresses, among others, the following non‑exhaustive objectives:

  • Identify and rigorously analyze complex problems (research problem) around a given topic, and resolve them through original, novel ideas (the thesis), intelligible and from verifiable sources.
  • Demonstrate, on a given topic, the ability to conduct a literature review and an inventory of documentary sources, to mobilize existing doctrine, and to collect and analyze all other empirical data.
  • Develop the ability to engage in structured and critical reasoning, to work autonomously, and to manage time.
  • Improve the ability to write and communicate scientifically.
  • Contribute to the advancement of knowledge through new knowledge useful to the scientific community and to society at large.
  • Publicly defend and publish research results in order to obtain the title of doctor.
  • Facilitate access to employment or improve professional careers.
Section 4

How to write your doctoral thesis

4.1 Formal elements

International writing standards

For all disciplines within the Humanities and Social Sciences at EDTM 3D, a doctoral thesis is written following the Chicago or Turabian style, with minor adjustments.

For disciplines in Engineering and Technology, Health Sciences, and Education Sciences, the standards in force are IMRaD and APA.

Structure of an EDTM 3D doctoral thesis

Cover pages:

  • Cover page following the EDTM 3D model (template in Appendix 1)
  • Blank page
  • Repeat of the cover page

Front matter:

  • Acknowledgements page (optional)
  • Abstract (in French) with main keywords
  • Abstract (in English) with keywords
  • Foreword (optional)
  • Summary table of contents
  • List of appendices (if applicable)
  • List of tables (if applicable)
  • List of figures, diagrams, and other illustrations (if applicable)
  • List of abbreviations, acronyms, and initialisms (depending on contents)
  • Glossary (as needed)

Body of the thesis:

  • Introduction
  • Developments structured into balanced divisions
  • Conclusion

Closing matter:

  • Bibliography (the webography appears under a separate heading)
  • Appendices
  • Thematic or general index (optional)
  • Table of contents

General presentation

  • Body text: Times New Roman, size 12, double line spacing (i.e. 1.5).
  • Footnotes: Times New Roman, size 9, single line spacing (i.e. 1).
  • Text must be justified for better readability.
  • Pages are numbered starting from the title page. Pagination is continuous and includes tables, figures, and appendices.
  • Arabic numerals start at the introduction and end, in principle, at the page dedicated to the bibliographic references. Pages before the introduction use a different format (for example, lowercase Roman numerals). The same applies to appendices or other elements following the table of contents (numbered, for example, in uppercase Roman numerals).

Length of the thesis

The length of the thesis varies by discipline.

4.2 Content elements

Writing should begin as early as possible in the doctoral process — but not before the research problem and the outline have been validated by the thesis director.

Each scientific discipline follows, by tradition, specific standards. For reference, here are the norms for the different domains at the Doctoral School 3D:

DomainNorms
Humanities and Social Sciences Two or three parts depending on the topic. Writing method: inductive or hypothetico‑deductive. Chicago or Turabian style.
Health Sciences and MedicineIMRaD; APA
Education SciencesIMRaD; APA
Engineering and Technology SciencesIMRaD; APA

Introduction

The introduction usually represents 3 – 5% of the total body text (Introduction + Development + Conclusion), excluding bibliography and appendices. It is written as several paragraphs but cannot be structured into titled subsections.

The introduction presents the following elements:

  • Opening hook (which may rely on a current event, a quotation, a maxim, etc.)
  • Presentation of the theme and definition of key terms
  • Presentation of the context in which the theme is situated (historical, ideological, economic, social, legal, political, etc.)
  • State of knowledge on the topic (brief summary of work already done on the subject, highlighting the salient points)
  • Statement of the interest or relevance of the topic
  • Research problem and main research question(s)
  • Stated hypotheses or main proposed solution
  • Objective of the study
  • Methodological approach adopted
  • Expected results of the study
  • Plan of the thesis, indicating exactly the title of each part (two or three parts depending on the topic)

Outline and subdivisions

For the Humanities and Social Sciences using the Chicago style, the number of parts varies between two and three depending on the discipline and the needs of the analysis. Legal analysis (in Law), for example, generally follows a binary structure with two main parts, whereas other disciplines such as sociology or geography adopt a ternary plan with three main parts.

Subdivisions of a binary plan:

  • Parts are divided in two
    • Each Part is divided into Titles (two)
      • Each Title is divided into Chapters (two)
        • Chapters are divided into Sections (two)
          • Sections are divided into Paragraphs (two)
            • Paragraphs may be subdivided into I, II, III, etc., themselves subdivided into A, B, C, etc.
              • If needed, further divisions may be used (1, 2, 3, etc., then a, b, c, etc.)

Subdivisions of a ternary plan:

  • Parts are divided in three
    • Each Part is divided into Titles (three)
      • Each Title is divided into Chapters (three)
        • Chapters are divided into Sections (three)
          • Sections are divided into Paragraphs (three)
            • Paragraphs may be subdivided into I, II, III, etc., themselves subdivided into A, B, C, etc.
              • If needed, further divisions may be used (1, 2, 3, etc., then a, b, c, etc.)

For Engineering and Technology, Education, and Health Sciences using the APA standard and IMRaD format, the outline follows the scientific approach:

  • Introduction — presents the observed phenomenon, the subject, and the research problem.
  • Materials and Methods — describes the type of method used and the tools, materials, and techniques mobilized to explore the problem.
  • Results — reports the research findings, which may be ordered by importance.
  • Discussion — compares the results with the initial hypotheses and examines confirmation or divergence, in order to draw conclusions.

The body text

  • Keep parts and subdivisions balanced in length.
  • Do not write in the first person ("I"). Prefer "we" or "one", and especially the impersonal mode: "it should be specified that…", "it is worth mentioning that…", "it is important to note that…", "it turns out that…", etc.
  • Mark the development of reasoning with logical connectors: "on the one hand, on the other hand"; "first, then, finally"; "in the first place, in the second place, lastly"; "moreover", "in addition", "nevertheless", "however", etc.
  • Do not address two or more ideas in the same paragraph. Conversely, one idea may be covered in one or more paragraphs. Avoid developing the same idea more than once — redundancy is prohibited. Writing a doctoral thesis is an evolving and non‑repetitive work of reflection.

Announcements and transitions

Each subdivision must be introduced by a chapeau — a short introduction summarizing the contents of the subdivision — and must end with a sentence (or paragraph) of conclusion and transition to the next part.

Citations and references

Every quotation taken from an outside source, as well as every borrowed idea, must be clearly distinguished.

When a direct quotation contains fewer than 40 words, it is a short direct quotation, inserted directly within the body text and delimited by quotation marks. If it contains 40 words or more, it is a long quotation: it appears as a separate block, indented by 1.27 cm, without quotation marks.

For the Humanities and Social Sciences, references are indicated by a superscript number in the text, pointing to a footnote. All citations and references are also repeated in a bibliography placed at the end of the document.

For Education Sciences, Engineering and Technology, and Health Sciences, all sources are given in parentheses and listed in the final bibliography.

Tables and figures

Tables and figures use continuous numbering throughout the body text. They are also fully listed in a "List of tables and figures" appearing in the table of contents.

The conclusion

The doctoral thesis ends with a conclusion of 5 – 8% of the body text (Introduction + Developments + Conclusion). The conclusion contains:

  • A reminder of the topic
  • A synthetic recap of the broad lines of the study
  • A reminder of the research problem and main questions
  • The main research findings
  • A reminder of the novel ideas and the theoretical or empirical contributions of the research
  • The limits of the study
  • Open perspectives and avenues for future research
Section 5

The bibliography

You must list in the bibliography every data source used in the footnotes (Chicago style) or in parenthetical citations (APA style). Conversely, never list a source that is not cited in the development or in the footnotes.

Sources are classified into a list subdivided by source type (general works, articles, doctoral theses, etc.). In all cases, books, articles, and other reference documents (reports, manuals, etc.) are sorted in ascending alphabetical order of author names. The same applies to webographic references.

Legal instruments, however, are listed from most recent to oldest, respecting the hierarchy of norms.

The reference list does not repeat itself: a reference is written only once.

The main standards followed at EDTM 3D are given in Appendix 2.

Section 6

The appendices

Appendices provide the reader with documents useful for supporting the arguments developed in the thesis or for completing the main information in the text.

They may include methodological materials such as interview grids or analysis protocols, thematic reports, exhaustive results (which would take too much space in the body text), interview transcripts, and so on.

Add a title to each appendix and refer to it in the developments. If appendices are very numerous, they may appear in a separate document.

Section 7

Plagiarism — a practice to ban

Plagiarism is strictly forbidden and constitutes a fault punishable by disciplinary sanctions, which may lead to the candidate's expulsion.

Plagiarism is the reproduction of a text, a part of a text, or an idea from another author without mentioning the source.

While the very nature of scientific research is to build on existing work, to discuss it and place it in perspective, the doctoral candidate has the ethical and intellectual duty to cite and reference every source they have drawn on.

Section 8

Generative AI tools — responsible use

EDTM 3D systematically checks for the use of generative artificial intelligence tools in the doctoral theses submitted to it.

While recognizing the assistance these tools provide — particularly for writing and formatting — candidates are expected to use them responsibly and in moderation.

The following principles apply at EDTM 3D:

  • Transparency. The use of AI must be explicitly and systematically identified in the work, in the same way as other sources.
  • Intellectual integrity. The use of AI must be limited to a research‑assistance function and must never replace the intellectual work required of the student.
  • Responsibility. Each student is fully responsible for their work — its accuracy, relevance, and currency. Respect for copyright and for personal or confidential information is part of this responsibility. Any erroneous, biased, invalid, or outdated information generated by a generative AI tool and used by the student will be entirely attributed to them.

These provisions are subject to change, in line with the institutional positions of the University of Toamasina and the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research.

References and citations

To help you with references and bibliography in line with APA and Chicago standards, several free resources are available online. You can browse them for an exhaustive list of bibliographic norms by source type.

Examples of online resources (in English and French):

  • boite-outils.bib.umontreal.ca/citer/chicago

  • pitt.libguides.com/citationhelp/turabian

Several free reference‑management tools (such as Zotero) generate and organize citations in every referencing style. No tool is 100% reliable, however — careful proofreading is always required.

Chicago / Turabian style (Humanities and Social Sciences)

Book

Footnote — first mention:

PatternFirstname Lastname, Title of the book, (Place of publication: Publisher, year), page(s).
ExampleGeorges Burdeau, Traité de science politique, (Paris: LGDJ, 1987), 10.

Footnote — subsequent mentions (short form):

PatternLastname, Title of the book, page(s).
ExampleBurdeau, Traité de science politique, 10.

Bibliography:

PatternLASTNAME, Firstname, Title of the work (in italics), publisher, edition number, volume, tome, place of publication, year of publication, number of pages.
ExampleBURDEAU, Georges, Traité de science politique, LGDJ, t. 2, Paris, 1987, 810 p.

Book chapter

Footnote — first mention:

PatternFirstname Lastname, "Title of the chapter", in Title of the edited book, ed. Firstname Lastname (Place: Publisher, year), page(s).
ExampleJennifer Martinez, "Leadership dans les environnements de travail à distance", in Gérer les organisations modernes, ed. Robert Williams (New York: Business Press, 2023), 156.

Footnote — subsequent mentions:

PatternLastname, "Title of the chapter", page(s).

Bibliography:

PatternLASTNAME, Firstname. "Title of the chapter". In Title of the edited book, edited by Firstname Lastname, chapter pages. Place: Publisher, year.
ExampleMARTINEZ, Jennifer. "Leadership dans les environnements de travail à distance". In Gérer les organisations modernes, edited by Robert Williams, 145–172. New York: Business Press, 2023.

Scientific journal article

Footnote — first mention:

PatternFirstname Lastname, "Title of the article", Journal title Volume, no. (year): page(s), URL or DOI (if online).

Bibliography:

PatternLASTNAME, Firstname, "Article title", Journal title (in italics), volume, number, year, pages of the article, specific page(s) cited if needed.

PatternLASTNAME, Firstname, "Title of the thesis", Institution name, year of final deposit, number of pages.

APA standards (Engineering and Technology, Education, Health Sciences)

In‑text citations

Whatever the type of document cited, the format remains the same: Lastname (author or organization), year of publication. Citations can be parenthetical or narrative.

Parenthetical citations. Indicate in parentheses the author's name and year of publication, separated by a comma.

Example(Bourdieu, 1979)

For two authors, names are separated by an ampersand (&).

Example(Quivy & Van Campenhoudt, 1995)

For three authors or more, only the first name is given, followed by "et al."

Example(Dupont et al., 2019)

Narrative citations. The author's name appears in the text and the date follows in parentheses.

ExampleBourdieu (1979) emphasizes that…

For two authors, use "and" in full.

ExampleAccording to Quivy and Van Campenhoudt (1995)…

For three or more, use "et al."

ExampleFor Dupont et al. (2019)…

Quoting verbatim

Give the author, year, and specific page after the quoted text. If the excerpt is fewer than 40 words, integrate it directly into the text, in quotation marks; final punctuation comes after the citation. If the excerpt has 40 words or more, present it as a separate paragraph without quotation marks, indented by 1.25 cm (one tab in Word). If it contains several paragraphs, the first line of each subsequent paragraph is indented by a further 1.25 cm. The citation is placed after the final punctuation of the excerpt.

Particularities of APA 7th edition

  • Footnotes are generally not used (with rare exceptions such as copyright mentions, or clarifications that would weigh down the text).
  • Latin phrases such as op. cit., idem, ibid. are not used. If a source is cited several times, APA prescribes repeating the citation.
  • Place of publication is no longer included in references for books and book chapters.
