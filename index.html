PROVA‑MG: A Probabilistic Routing Framework for Low‑Resource Malagasy Speech Recognition

A novel, unified methodology for Automatic Speech Recognition that aggregates heterogeneous foundation models, typological priors, and a large licensed unlabeled corpus under explicit calibration and explicit constraints. With a complete validation plan to prove that it works.

Part One

The existing methodological landscape

The question of novelty is not "which algorithm" but "at which layer of the stack does the contribution live, and what does it claim". The field is best read as ten layers, each largely settled.

1.1 The classical layer

The HMM‑GMM paradigm modeled speech as a hidden Markov chain emitting Gaussian mixtures over short‑frame features (MFCC, PLP). It introduced the decomposition P(W|X) proportional to P(X|W) P(W) that still underlies any probabilistic reading of ASR, and weighted finite‑state transducer decoding that remains the formal substrate of beam search. No thesis can be "about" this layer today, but any thesis that ignores it is theoretically thin.

1.2 The hybrid layer (DNN‑HMM, 2009 to 2016)

Deep neural networks replaced the Gaussian mixture as the emission model while the decoder, lexicon, and language model stayed external. Kaldi chain models, TDNN‑F, and lattice‑free MMI live here. This regime is engineering‑heavy and unforgiving on data.

1.3 The end‑to‑end layer (2014 to 2020)

Three objectives still structure the field. CTC (Graves et al. 2006) marginalizes over all monotonic alignments and is the dominant choice when data is scarce because it converges fast with few hyperparameters. Attention encoder‑decoder (Listen, Attend and Spell, Chan et al. 2016) drops the monotonic assumption and is more expressive but more data‑hungry. RNN‑Transducer (Graves 2012) reintroduces monotonic alignment with an internal predictor and is the production choice for streaming. Conformer (Gulati et al. 2020) is the encoder that combines convolution for local patterns with self‑attention for long range context.

1.4 The self‑supervised representation layer (2018 to 2022)

Wav2Vec 2.0 (Baevski et al. 2020) introduced contrastive self‑supervised pre‑training on unlabeled audio; HuBERT (Hsu et al. 2021) replaced the contrastive term with masked prediction over discretized targets; WavLM added denoising; data2vec unified modalities under self‑distillation. Fine‑tuned with a small supervised head, these representations often match models trained from scratch on far more data.

1.5 The cross‑lingual self‑supervised layer (2020 to 2024)

XLSR‑53 and XLS‑R (Babu et al. 2022, 436,000 hours, 128 languages), MMS (Pratap et al. 2023, over 1,100 languages), and Wav2Vec2‑BERT (Seamless Communication 2024, roughly 4.5 million hours, 143 languages) produce universal acoustic representations. Malagasy is nominally covered by all of them; in practice the zero‑shot quality is poor.

1.6 The large‑scale weak supervision layer (2022 to present)

Whisper (Radford et al. 2023) trained on 680,000 hours of audio paired with weakly supervised web transcripts. USM, Canary, OWSM, and OLMoASR are variants and open reimplementations. Whisper‑large‑v3 currently "knows the most" about Malagasy of any open release, which is why the SIGUL pipeline uses it for first‑pass pseudo‑labels.

1.7 The parameter‑efficient adaptation layer

LoRA, adapters, BitFit, and prompt tuning specialize large encoders by training a small fraction of parameters. For low‑resource ASR this matters both for GPU budget and for reducing overfitting on small supervised sets.

1.8 The semi‑supervised and pseudo‑labeling layer

slimIPL, Kaizen, and Noisy Student Training share one skeleton: train a seed model, transcribe a large unlabeled set, filter by confidence, retrain on the union, repeat. The variations concern filtering, teacher update, and collapse prevention. The procedure works empirically but its conditions of success are not formally characterized.

1.9 The speech‑LLM integration layer (2023 to present)

Recent work feeds speech to a large language model, either as discrete tokens or through a learned projection (SALMONN, Qwen‑Audio). Quality on well‑resourced languages rivals dedicated ASR; on low‑resource languages the bottleneck shifts to the LLM's text coverage.

1.10 The data and licensing layer

This is the layer the existing SIGUL draft occupies: license‑first data construction, segment‑level traceability, datasheets, reproducibility. It is genuinely contributive, but it is a methodology for constructing a corpus, not for modeling speech. Necessary but not sufficient for a thesis whose center is "neural and probabilistic approaches".

Part Two

Where the gaps actually are

Several methods are "taken", in the sense that completed theses or near‑thesis bodies of work already exist for them: continued self‑supervised pre‑training for a single low‑resource language, pseudo‑labeling with confidence filtering, LoRA and adapter cross‑lingual fine‑tuning, dialect‑conditioned adapters, and code‑switched ASR. Doing any of these for Malagasy would be linguistically new but not methodologically new.

What is genuinely under‑explored at thesis scale:

  • The principled probabilistic integration of multiple heterogeneous foundation models treated as distinct posteriors, with their disagreement structure used as a routing signal rather than discarded.
  • The injection of typological priors from a known language family as Bayesian priors that constrain the hypothesis space, rather than as soft cues for data augmentation.
  • The formal treatment of iterative pseudo‑labeling as a constrained variational EM procedure with explicit safeguards against model collapse, with convergence analysis.
  • The unification of dialect, register, and code‑switching as latent variables in one graphical model with explicit conditional independence structure.
The thesis claim Each item above is a thinkable Interspeech paper. Their combination, applied coherently to one under‑resourced language with a corpus you built yourself under a license‑first regime, is a thinkable PhD thesis.
Part Three

The novel methodology: PROVA‑MG

Working name: PROVA‑MG, for Probabilistic Routing Of Variational Alignments for Malagasy. Five interlocking components. The thesis formalizes each individually, then shows that their integration beats any subset.

Component 3.1

Heterogeneous Foundation Model Co‑Inference

Whisper‑large‑v3, MMS, and Wav2Vec2‑BERT are treated not as competing baselines but as three approximate posteriors over the transcript. Each encodes different inductive biases (weak supervision, religious‑text alignment, self‑supervision), so their disagreement on a Malagasy segment is informative.

For each unlabeled segment x we compute approximate posteriors and define a learned routing function:

r : ( q_W(y|x), q_M(y|x), q_B(y|x) ) → { train_high_confidence , route_to_human , hold_in_reserve , discard }

The routing function is trained on the small supervised set, using the agreement structure as features. We route, we do not average: averaging is Bayes‑optimal only when teachers are mutually calibrated, and these are not.

Component 3.2

Typologically Informed Bayesian Priors

Malagasy belongs to the Malayo‑Polynesian subgroup; its phonotactics and morphology overlap measurably with Indonesian, Malay, Cebuano, and Tagalog, which have far more data. We train a phonotactic and morphotactic prior on Austronesian text and incorporate it into decoding via a calibrated mixture:

P(y|x) ∝ P_acoustic(x|y) · P_LM_MG(y)^α · P_typ(y)^β

The hypothesis: this prior regularizes regions of the hypothesis space that a Malagasy‑only language model cannot constrain because of data sparsity, but a family‑level prior can.

Component 3.3

Constrained Variational EM for Iterative Pseudo‑Labeling

On the extended dataset D = D_sup union D_unsup, parameters θ and latent transcripts are inferred jointly:

E‑step:  q*(ỹ|x) = argmin_q KL( q || p_θ(ỹ|x) ) subject to constraints C M‑step:  θ ← argmax_θ E_q*[ log p_θ(y_sup, ỹ_unsup | x) ] + λ R(θ)

The constraints C are the novelty: a confidence floor, a KL divergence bound on drift between successive iterations, an agreement constraint from 3.1, and a typological consistency constraint from 3.2. Under these constraints the thesis derives convergence and non‑collapse conditions that the existing empirical literature does not articulate.

Component 3.4

Latent Variable Decomposition: Dialect, Register, Code‑Switching

The generation process is decomposed into a graphical model with explicit latent variables:

x → ( d , r , c , y ) d = dialect   r = register   c = per‑segment code‑switching language identity

The acoustic model factorizes as p(x|y,d,r), the language model as p(y|d,c,r). Dialect‑specific adapters condition on d, register parameters on r, code‑switched segments route through a French acoustic model. Threads usually treated as separate subproblems become one generative model admitting joint inference.

Component 3.5

Linguistically Informed Confidence Calibration

ASR models are overconfident; mis‑calibrated confidences sabotage the routing of 3.1 and the constraints of 3.3. A small post‑hoc calibration model (temperature scaling or isotonic regression) is trained on the supervised set, with features including model entropy, three‑way teacher agreement, typological likelihood, segment duration, voice‑activity confidence, and dialect‑posterior entropy. The calibrated confidence is what every downstream constraint actually consumes.

The unifying claim Low‑resource ASR is best approached not as one model on one objective with one source of supervision, but as a probabilistic system that aggregates heterogeneous evidence under explicit calibration and explicit constraints. Malagasy is the empirical demonstration.
Part Four

Why this is not someone else's thesis

Each component touches existing literature, but none has been done in this form, and the integration has not been done at all.

Part Five

Integration with the existing project

Nothing already committed is discarded. PROVA‑MG absorbs it.

  • The license‑first pipeline becomes the data substrate: it produces the gold supervised set and the large licensed unlabeled set, plus the dialect, register, and license metadata that feed the latent‑variable model of 3.4.
  • The planned continued pre‑training of Wav2Vec2‑BERT produces one of the three teachers in 3.1.
  • The planned architecture comparison is repurposed: instead of choosing one winner, three teachers are kept and co‑infer; the comparison calibrates the routing function.
  • The planned pseudo‑labeling loop becomes the constrained EM of 3.3, with safeguards made explicit.
  • The planned dialect adaptation becomes 3.4, with dialect as a latent variable.

Part Six

Risks and honesty

  • Scope. Five components is ambitious. A defensible thesis is 3.1 + 3.3 + 3.5, with 3.2 and 3.4 as future work if time runs short.
  • Theoretical depth. Components 3.3 and 3.5 need honest variational inference, EM convergence, and calibration theory. Strong probabilistic supervision (ideally a co‑director) is advisable; if unavailable, scope 3.3 to empirical claims with sketched proofs.
  • Empirical uncertainty. Typological priors (3.2) may not move WER if the Malagasy LM is strong. The ablation is essential, and a negative result is itself publishable.
  • Compute. Three teachers at scale is expensive. Mitigation: distill the co‑inference into a single student once the corpus exists; teachers are needed at training time, not deployment.
  • Novelty contestability. Publish early. SIGUL anchors priority on data; a fast Interspeech submission on 3.1 anchors priority on methodology.
Part Seven

Validation checklist: every work item to prove it

A complete, ordered work plan. Each phase ends with a gate: a measurable result that must hold before the next phase is worth starting. Check items off as they are done; progress is saved automatically.

Validation progress 0 / 0 complete
P0

Foundations, infrastructure, and evaluation harness

You cannot prove anything without a trustworthy measuring stick. Build it first.

Gate 0   Baselines reproduce within a small margin of published WER, and the harness gives stable confidence intervals on a repeated run.
P1

Data substrate (license-first pipeline)

PROVA-MG consumes a gold supervised set and a large licensed unlabeled set. The pipeline produces both.

Gate 1   A licensed unlabeled pool large enough for pseudo-labeling exists, and the supervised set is large enough to train a routing function and a calibration head.
P2

Component 3.1 — Heterogeneous Foundation Model Co-Inference

Prove that routing across distinct teachers beats any single teacher, averaging, and voting.

Gate 2   Routing yields a statistically significant WER reduction over the best single teacher AND over averaging, on at least two test sets. If not, the disagreement signal is not informative and 3.1 must be revised.
P3

Component 3.5 — Confidence Calibration

Built early because 3.1 routing and 3.3 constraints both consume calibrated confidence.

Gate 3   Calibrated confidence has substantially lower ECE than raw confidence and improves routing decisions; the calibrator generalizes from supervised to unlabeled distribution.
P4

Component 3.2 — Typologically Informed Priors

Prove that an Austronesian family prior constrains decoding where a Malagasy-only LM cannot.

Gate 4   Either the typological prior gives a measurable, localized WER gain (component confirmed) or it does not (component becomes a documented negative result; 3.2 dropped from the load-bearing path).
P5

Component 3.3 — Constrained Variational EM

The theoretically heaviest component. Prove convergence, prove non-collapse, and prove the constraints matter.

Gate 5   The constrained loop improves WER monotonically (or near-monotonically) over iterations, the unconstrained loop demonstrably collapses, and each constraint has a measurable effect.
P6

Component 3.4 — Latent Dialect, Register, Code-Switching

Prove that joint inference of the latent variables beats treating them as separate subproblems.

Gate 6   Joint inference reduces WER on under-represented dialects and code-switched speech without degrading the majority dialect.
P7

Full-system validation and the thesis claim

Prove that the integrated system beats every subset and every published baseline.

Gate 7 (the thesis claim)   The integrated PROVA-MG system significantly outperforms every ablation and every baseline on independent, frozen, dialect-stratified test sets, and the gain is attributable to the integration rather than to any single component alone.
