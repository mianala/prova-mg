State of the art (II): low‑resource and multilingual ASR

This chapter surveys the methods that extend the general ASR paradigms to low‑resource and multilingual settings, then identifies the open methodological problems that the present work addresses.

Part Two — A

Multilingual and low‑resource methods

The four paradigms reviewed in Chapter 1 do not by themselves close the gap between high‑resource and low‑resource languages. The following six families of methods address that gap; Malagasy is nominally covered by most of them, yet zero‑shot performance remains poor.

2.1 Cross‑lingual self‑supervised representations

XLSR‑53 and XLS‑R (Babu et al., 2022, 436 000 h, 128 languages), MMS (Pratap et al., 2023, more than 1 100 languages), and Wav2Vec2‑BERT (Seamless Communication, 2024, approximately 4.5 M h, 143 languages) produce universal acoustic representations. Although Malagasy is included in all of them, the published zero‑shot word error rate (WER) remains high.

2.2 Large‑scale weak supervision

Whisper (Radford et al., 2023) was trained on 680 000 h of audio paired with weakly supervised web transcripts. USM, Canary, OWSM, and OLMoASR are variants and open reimplementations. Whisper‑large‑v3 currently produces the strongest open zero‑shot transcripts for Malagasy and is therefore used as a first‑pass teacher in the present pipeline.

2.3 Parameter‑efficient adaptation

LoRA, adapters, BitFit, and prompt tuning specialize large encoders by training a small fraction of parameters. For low‑resource ASR these methods reduce both the GPU budget and the risk of overfitting on small supervised sets.

2.4 Semi‑supervised learning and pseudo‑labeling

slimIPL, Kaizen, and Noisy Student Training share a common procedure: a seed model is trained, it transcribes a large unlabeled set, the resulting pseudo‑labels are filtered by confidence, and the model is retrained on the union. Variations concern the filtering criterion, the teacher‑update rule, and the prevention of collapse. The procedure works empirically but its conditions of success are not formally characterized in the literature.

2.5 Speech‑LLM integration

Recent systems feed speech to a large language model either as discrete tokens or through a learned projection (SALMONN, Qwen‑Audio). For high‑resource languages, quality rivals dedicated ASR; for low‑resource languages, performance is limited by the text‑side coverage of the underlying LLM.

2.6 Data construction and licensing

License‑first data construction, segment‑level traceability, datasheets, and reproducibility have become a research contribution in their own right. They constitute the data substrate of the present work but do not, by themselves, constitute a modeling methodology.

Part Two — B

Open methodological problems

Several methods are well established at thesis scale: continued self‑supervised pre‑training for a single low‑resource language, pseudo‑labeling with confidence filtering, LoRA and adapter cross‑lingual fine‑tuning, dialect‑conditioned adapters, and code‑switched ASR. Applying any of these to Malagasy would be linguistically informative but would not constitute a methodological contribution.

The following problems, by contrast, remain open at thesis scale and structure the methodology proposed in Chapter 3:

  • The principled probabilistic integration of multiple heterogeneous foundation models, treated as distinct posteriors and exploiting their disagreement structure as a routing signal.
  • The injection of typological priors from a known language family as Bayesian priors that constrain the decoding hypothesis space.
  • The formal treatment of iterative pseudo‑labeling as a constrained variational EM procedure with explicit non‑collapse conditions and convergence analysis.
  • The unification of dialect, register, and code‑switching as latent variables in a single graphical model with explicit conditional independence structure.
Transition These four problems, taken together and instantiated on a single under‑resourced language with a corpus constructed under a license‑first regime, define the scope of the methodology developed in the next chapter.
