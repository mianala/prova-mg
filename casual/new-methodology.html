So, what’s the new methodology?

A short, plain‑language answer to the question I keep getting asked. No equations, no Greek letters — just the idea, the way I’d explain it over coffee.

The short version

One sentence

Instead of training one big model on the tiny bit of Malagasy data we have, I treat the problem as a system: I let three different pre‑trained models “vote”, I lean on related languages to fill the gaps, and I only trust the labels that survive a few sanity checks. That’s it — everything else is bookkeeping.

Why bother

The problem, plainly

Malagasy is technically “supported” by every big multilingual speech model out there — Whisper, MMS, Wav2Vec2‑BERT — but if you actually try them, the transcripts are rough. We don’t have enough clean labelled audio to train our own from scratch, and just fine‑tuning one of the big models doesn’t fix it either. So the question is: how do you squeeze better Malagasy ASR out of what we already have?

The idea

Five moving parts

The framework I’m calling PROVA‑MG has five pieces. They sound fancy on paper but each one is really doing something simple:

1. Let the big models disagree — and use the disagreement

I run three foundation models on the same audio. When all three agree, I’m pretty confident the transcript is right. When they disagree, that’s a signal — either the audio is hard, or one of the models is wrong, or it’s a dialect/register thing. A small classifier decides what to do with each segment: keep it, send it to a human, hold it for later, or throw it away.

2. Borrow from cousin languages

Malagasy is Austronesian. Indonesian, Malay, Tagalog, Cebuano — same family, way more text data. So I train a small language model on all of them together, and use it as a soft prior during decoding. It nudges the system toward transcripts that “sound like” an Austronesian language should sound, even when the Malagasy‑only model has never seen the word before.

3. Pseudo‑label, but carefully

The classic trick with unlabeled audio is: have your model transcribe it, then train on its own transcripts. The problem is the model gets cocky and the errors snowball. I do the same loop, but I refuse to add a transcript unless it passes four checks — calibrated confidence is high enough, two out of three teachers agree, the typological prior likes it, and the model isn’t drifting too fast iteration to iteration. That’s the “constrained EM” in the long version.

4. Treat dialect, register, and code‑switching as part of the model

Malagasy isn’t one thing. Merina, Betsimisaraka, Sakalava, Antandroy — different sounds, different vocab. And people switch into French mid‑sentence all the time. Instead of pretending it’s one homogeneous language, the model jointly figures out the dialect, the register (read, broadcast, conversational, ceremonial), and which bits are French — alongside the transcript itself. Each one helps the others.

5. Make sure the confidence numbers mean something

ASR models always say they’re 95% sure. They’re not. So I train a small calibration head on the labelled set, just to map the raw scores to honest probabilities. Once those numbers are trustworthy, every other piece — the routing, the pseudo‑label filter — actually works.

What makes it new

Why this is different

None of the individual ingredients are brand new. People have used foundation models, pseudo‑labeling, typological priors, calibration. What I’m claiming is the combination: the five pieces aren’t a checklist, they feed each other. The calibrator feeds the router and the EM constraints. The typological prior feeds one of the constraints. The teacher agreement feeds another. The latent dialect/register/code‑switching adapters get used by all the other components. Pull one out and the rest get weaker.

The other thing is that I’m being explicit about when it can fail. Pseudo‑labeling collapses if you’re not careful — I write down the four conditions under which mine provably doesn’t. Most papers just say “and then we iterated three times and it worked.”

What I’m not claiming

Honest limits

It’s not magic. If you have zero labelled Malagasy, this doesn’t bootstrap from nothing — the calibrator and the routing classifier both need the small supervised set. It’s also compute‑heavy: running three foundation models on a big unlabeled pool is the part that dominates the budget. And until I actually run the experiments in Chapter IV, the numbers are expected results, not measured ones.
